AI in Recruiting: Same Tool, Two Countries

A branching scenario about bringing AI into hiring. You play Luana, Head of Talent at a Brazilian fintech that takes its AI recruiting tool to Stuttgart, from a lightly regulated market into a highly regulated one. Every feature of the tool has to clear two levels: does it make sense? and is it legal? It takes about 20 minutes. Short on time? Jump to the designer’s notes.

After this scenario you can:

  • judge whether an AI recruiting feature pays off before asking whether it’s legal,
  • tell which features are prohibited, conditional or allowed in the EU, what Brazil already requires, and why the rules exist,
  • recognise face and voice as biometric data in both countries,
  • set up human oversight that counts, and answer a candidate who asks whether an AI rejected them,
  • argue for one standard across both countries.

        Designer’s notes

        Who it’s for

        Recruiting and HR teams at companies that are expanding into new countries. The scenario raises awareness that AI in hiring is regulated very differently across borders: a tool that is common and legal at home can be prohibited abroad, or need the works council’s agreement before it’s switched on. It doesn’t turn recruiters into lawyers. It shows them where to stop and ask before a tool travels with the company. It works from both sides of the border: Brazilian teams get a look at rules that may be coming their way, and teams in the EU see their own rules through an outsider’s eyes.

        The problem

        In Brazil, many recruiting teams use AI that would be a problem in Germany: video interviews scored for “enthusiasm”, WhatsApp voice interviews that knock candidates out by keyword, automatic rejections, profiling. The usual training lists the rules country by country. It skips two questions that come first: does the tool actually make hiring better, and why do the rules exist at all?

        Why this format

        • Two levels. Every feature has to pass “Does it make sense?” before “Is it legal?”, and the sums are checked again after Level 2, because a legal duty is a cost too.
        • A meter that tells the truth. “Days to offer” is what the dashboard shows; “Days to a good hire” only appears after Level 1. The gap between them answers the “AI means faster hiring” myth through play rather than a lecture.
        • Look under the label. In the vendor call, learners choose three of six questions. The right ones reveal that a “confidence” score is emotion recognition.
        • Oversight as a task. Learners review three rejected applicants themselves, and one of the rejections is fair. Oversight means checking, not overruling by reflex.
        • Why, not only what. Every legal decision comes with the harm the rule prevents and a real case, from a broadcaster’s test of a video-interview AI to research on speech recognition and stuttering.
        • Consequences and confidence. Decisions carry forward to one of six endings, and learners rate how sure they were after each decision, as in my EU AI Act module.

        Where it fits: the learning program

        The scenario is the first step of a short program for a company that is taking its recruiting into a new country. It builds awareness. The decisions are made in the workshop that follows.

        1. The scenario: awareness (self-paced, about 20 minutes). For everyone in recruiting and HR who chooses, runs or reviews AI tools. Learners leave with the two questions to ask about any feature, “Does it make sense?” and “Is it legal?”, and a sense of where the new country’s rules differ from home. In a real rollout, their confidence ratings show the workshop team where the group was most sure and most wrong.
        2. Tool inventory: pre-work. Each tool owner lists the AI tools and features the team uses today: what each one decides, what data it reads (CVs, video, voice), which countries it runs in and when its contract is up.
        3. Deep-dive workshop: decisions. Recruiting and HR sit down with legal, compliance and the Head or VP of People & Talent. Together they take every feature from the inventory through the scenario’s two questions: does it make sense, and is it legal in every country it will run in? Each feature leaves the room with one decision: keep, expand (to more roles or countries), ditch, or switch (to another tool, or back to a person). Because the people who own the risk and the person who owns the budget are in the same room, decisions don’t wait for a second round. The output is a decision list per tool and country, with a reason at each level, an owner and a date.

        Who I’d build it with

        In a real project, I wouldn’t build this alone.

        • The scenario, with legal and compliance. They decide what counts as the right answer on every legal question, sign off the verdicts and flag when the law changes.
        • The workshop, with legal, compliance and the Head or VP of People & Talent. Legal and compliance bring the risk view. The Head or VP of People & Talent owns the hiring targets and the tool budget, and makes sure the decisions are carried out.

        This portfolio version was built without those partners. I checked the legal points against the law texts myself (see Status below).

        Status

        The legal points were checked against the text of the AI Act, the GDPR, German law (BetrVG, AGG) and Brazilian law (LGPD, Lei 9.029) as of November 2026. The story, the companies, the tool and its numbers are fictional. This interaction was not reviewed by legal experts and is meant to be a simple portfolio piece. It is not meant to be legal advice in any form.

        What I’d measure in a real rollout

        • Level 1 judgement: how many learners keep features that don’t pay off, and whether that changes on replay.
        • Calibration: the share of very confident but wrong answers, especially on emotion recognition and automated decisions.
        • Transfer: whether every feature in the workshop inventory leaves with a decision (keep, expand, ditch or switch) and a reason at both levels.
        • Behaviour: time to a good hire and early leavers in the months after a rollout, not only time to offer.
