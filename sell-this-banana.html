Sell this Banana

A short pixel-art game for junior salespeople. The scenes are silly and the skills are real: you sell a banana, then use the same skill back at the office, where you sell Karl-Klein 2.0, a fictional German language model. This prototype has Levels 1 and 2 of 6, about five minutes each. Short on time? Jump to the designer’s notes.

After Levels 1 and 2 you can:

  • name the four steps of a simple sale: need, offer, price, ask,
  • recognise a buying signal and ask for the sale instead of talking on,
  • answer a “maybe later” by leaving the door open, not by pushing,
  • use open questions and follow-ups to find the real need behind “just looking”,
  • turn a feature into a benefit and a value for one specific buyer.

Designer’s notes

Who it’s for

Junior salespeople in their first months, who need solid basics and don’t know the product yet. The product here is Karl-Klein 2.0, a fictional small language model trained and run in Germany. It isn’t as strong as Claude or Gemini, and it doesn’t try to be: its selling point is sovereignty, independence from US and Chinese providers, for buyers who work with sensitive data.

Why a game

Sales basics are easy to agree with and hard to do under pressure. A silly scene takes the pressure off, so learners try the wrong thing on purpose and see what happens. Then each level ends back at the office, where the same skill meets the real product in one decision.

How Level 1 teaches

  • Ripeness is the turn budget. Every line costs, and long speeches cost three times as much. Talking past a buying signal shows up on the bunch instead of in a lecture.
  • Trust drops when you push. Pushing a bunch of twelve on someone who wants one, or pressing after “maybe later”, costs trust. At zero, the customer walks.
  • A delayed consequence. The jogger who hears “I’m here till five” comes back and buys. In sales, results often show up later, and the game makes that visible.
  • Fails are collectable. Each fail card says what happened, why it fails in real sales and what to try. Finding them on purpose is part of the game.
  • Transfer in one decision. At the office, each wrong reply to the email mirrors one of the fails on the beach.

How Level 2 teaches

  • Questions have a budget. Each one costs ripeness. Open questions reveal need cards, closed ones get short answers, and leading ones cost trust and reveal nothing.
  • The first answer isn’t the real need. “I’m running on empty” is the surface. Follow-up questions only appear after an answer, and digging leads to the real need: cramps in the last set. The rating rewards the dig: one banana for answering any need, two for the real one, three for finding it in five questions without leading.
  • The pitch is a chain, built in three picks. First the need, then the one feature that answers it, then the words. Some features are true but don’t matter to this buyer (the price, the gym bag). Pure facts and big claims don’t land either.
  • Transfer to a real call. At the office, learners pick three discovery questions for a law firm, then build feature → benefit → value for Karl-Klein 2.0.

What comes next

Four more levels, each with one skill: a cold call on a high-speed train, objections next to the MegaBanana stall, a buying committee in a hospital kitchen, and a boss level at a ministry, where proof counts more than persuasion. Each level adds one rule to a sales notebook, and the six rules become a cheat sheet at the end. In a real project, a product owner would confirm the product facts and a sales expert would review the sales practice.

Status

Prototype, Levels 1 and 2 of 6. Everything is fictional: the product, its maker, the customers and the prices. The pixel art is drawn in code, the music is original and synthesized in the browser (off until you turn it on), and the game saves nothing in your browser.
