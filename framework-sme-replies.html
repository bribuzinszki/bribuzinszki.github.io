How I get SMEs to reply to me

Seven steps, from the first coffee chat to calling in the big guns.

Missing Subject Matter Expert
Last seen:

Answers to:
“quick question?”
If found, please reply.

You join a new company, you get your first task – and then you sit on it for a good while, because nobody replies to you. It’s as if something had wiped out all the subject matter experts (SMEs) who were supposed to let their wisdom rain down on the masterpiece you’re creating.

Still, customers need to be enabled, playbooks need to be updated and infographics need to be corrected. So how do we get our SMEs talking to us?

Here is my approach, in short. I’m not claiming universal truth, nor am I saying this is the perfect way to go about it. I’m only saying that these steps have served me pretty well for the last decade or so. That’s why I want to share them with you, as a last resort before you file a missing person’s report for your SMEs.

Two ways to use this page: starting a new project? Follow the route from step 1. Already waiting on a silent SME? Take the quick check below and jump straight to the step you need.

Already waiting on a reply?

Four yes/no questions, about 30 seconds. I’ll point you to the step to try next.

Phase 1

Before you ask

Lay the groundwork before you need anything.

Step 1 of 7Build a personal relationship

When joining a new company, I always recommend taking the first couple of weeks to schedule coffee chats with as many people as possible, your future SMEs among them. If you enable customers, those will probably be the product managers, the product and solution engineers, and your customer success team. Since your enablement piece will be customer-facing, you also want to be on very good terms with marketing (copy and design).

Companies are busy places. People always need to prioritize, and when push comes to shove, you don’t want to be the meeting that gets cut or the message that’s left unanswered. How important your enablement piece is will vary from SME to SME, so make sure your own importance stays constant:

Be a human first, and a learning experience designer second.

Step 2 of 7Have a wingman

Okay, but we can’t always be friends with everyone, or you haven’t yet had the chance to build a relationship with this specific person. This is where your first weeks of coffee chats and your solid knowledge of who gets along with whom come into play.

Ask someone you know has a good relationship with the SME to put in a word for you, or to introduce you and join your first couple of meetings or Slack conversations. Your wingman usually turns out to be someone who has been at the company long enough to know most people, and who you truly get along with.

From the fieldwho makes a good wingman

Seniority has nothing to do with whether someone makes a good wingman. Some of my best wingmen were juniors, young and full of energy. Time at the company, however, is usually a good indicator of wingman quality. And odds are, if you like the person, others like them too.

Step 3 of 7Get proper buy-in

The project is green-lit, you got buy-in for your work! But did you really?

Your manager or a certain department might position your project as important, but odds are the managers of your SMEs haven’t even heard of it, or have only heard of it in passing.

Especially at start-ups, communication can be erratic. It happens at the water cooler at odd times or in private Slack messages, and one important piece of information gets buried under the next crisis. Enablement only becomes important on onboarding or launch day. Until then, you’re a footnote, something that can be pushed to tomorrow, and you don’t live at the forefront of people’s minds.

This is why it’s good etiquette to follow proper project management protocol and make sure all your stakeholders, your SMEs’ managers included, get a short memo on your project: what it is, when it happens and how much of your SMEs’ time it will take away from their other work.

Have the managers approve that time in advance. I’ve often seen SMEs unsure whether they were even allowed to prioritize my project. Approval from above puts their minds at ease and makes them feel better about taking those 30 minutes to green-light your script.

Phase 2

When you ask

Make your request the easiest thing on their list.

Step 4 of 7Make it easy

We all love an easy task first thing in the morning: something we can quickly knock off our endless to-do list that makes us feel productive. Wouldn’t it be great if your ask served that purpose? If your request is the easy thing your SMEs can get through quickly, you’re more likely to get a reply.

But how do we make it easy for our experts?

Answering is easier than explaining

Answering is easier than explaining

Read and learn as much as you can about the product or topic before you kick off the conversation with your SMEs. You don’t have to become an expert yourself, but you should know the material well enough to ask clarifying questions. It’s okay to ask for good sources, so you know you’re learning from the right material, but your SME shouldn’t be your primary source of information. They are there to clear up doubts and point you in the right direction.

Approving is easier than ideating

Approving is easier than ideating

Instead of asking for ideas and topics for a curriculum, come with a draft and ask them to approve it. Odds are they will want to add some things and take out or highlight others, but it’s easier to start from something than from an empty page. You don’t have to get it right the first time, but you have to put in the effort to get close.

Correcting is easier than drafting

Correcting is easier than drafting

Take the sources your SME pointed you to and the answers they gave you, and start drafting. Ideally, you work in a modular way: what you learn from your SME’s corrections on the first module, you carry over to the second, which in turn makes your SME’s job easier. This way, “a quick read” really is quick enough for them to want to do it.

Again, and this is important: you don’t have to get it right the first time, but you do have to do the drafting and redrafting on your own. Your SME is the eye that spots the flaws only an expert could. They are not your co-writer (unless they want to be – in that case, take that very rare offer and cherish the experience).

Step 5 of 7Set clear expectations

You asked for a reply, but you didn’t say by when. Now your ask sits behind all the other asks that came with a deadline.

You asked for feedback, but you didn’t say what it should look like. Now you might be stuck with one-liners like “Dive deeper on this topic” or “Too long, make it shorter”. (If that has already happened to you, I have a framework for that: the SME Feedback Framework.)

All of this costs time, because you’ll be busy writing reminders and asking for clarifications. Compare these two asks:

Clear expectations on the time it takes, what feedback should look like, the deadline and any other asks make it easier for your SME to act, and save you time and frustration.

From the fieldafraid of sounding pushy?

Some people shy away from clear expectations because they feel pushy or demanding. Don’t. Set those deadlines, and cut people some slack when they can’t meet them. A missed deadline is your clue that something else is going on: have a conversation, offer help, or work out another way to reach the goal you have to hit.

Phase 3

When they go quiet

Still quiet? Time to step it up.

Step 6 of 7Go where the people are

So, you’ve done everything above, and your SME is still dragging their feet.

Well, buckle up: you’re going on a field trip to where your SME is!

This works best in hybrid or on-site settings: catch your SME during a lunch break or at the water cooler. People feel uncomfortable looking you in the eye when they know they’ve been brushing you off. A short reminder that yes, you do indeed exist, and no, you’re not mad, just slightly disappointed, often does the trick. Watch that magical message appear in your DMs the same day or the next, and you’re back on track.

From the fieldhow often I’ve needed this

I’ve only needed this twice, both times during chaotic periods at the companies in question. People had to prioritize in tough ways, and enablement is always the first thing to slide down the priority list. All it took was a quick lunch and a few niceties, and the ball was rolling again.

Step 7 of 7The last resort

But what if you work remotely, or that SME works from another office, and you’re running out of ideas?

Well, you call in the big guns: you ask your manager to talk to your SME’s manager.

Ideally, that conversation happened before the project was even green-lit. Sometimes, though, it takes another quick exchange at a higher level to put priorities into perspective. When SMEs don’t reply, it’s usually because their manager has given them other tasks with tighter deadlines, or has forgotten to factor in the time allocated to your project. A quick reminder between two peers often solves the issue.

Your manager can also help you understand why communication is tougher than usual, since they have a better overview of what’s going on across the company. That might give you new ideas on how to approach the matter and find a way of working that fits this specific project.

I hope these tips helped you find your SMEs again. If so, congrats! If not, you might need higher powers than I can conjure up here.

Either way, happy building!
