Grounding AI with RAG

An interactive learning piece on Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). In about five minutes, you see how a chatbot's answer changes when it is grounded in a company document, and why you still need to check the source. Short on time? Jump to the designer’s notes.

After this exercise you can:

  • tell a general LLM reply apart from a RAG-grounded reply,
  • switch on grounding so an LLM answers from your documents,
  • check a grounded reply against its source to catch hallucinations.

What to take away

  1. Spot the difference. Without grounding, an LLM answers from general training data. The reply sounds confident, but it is generic, has no source, and can be made up for your company.
  2. Turn grounding on. For company-specific questions, use a RAG-enabled tool or attach the relevant document (here: the Grounding switch). A grounded answer should tell you where it came from.
  3. A citation is not proof. RAG reduces hallucinations but does not remove them. Open the source and check the details that matter: numbers, dates, conditions and exceptions. Watch out for citations that point to a section that doesn't say what the answer claims.

Designer’s notes

Who it’s for

Employees who use an AI assistant at work and have no technical background. It’s designed as a short, standalone piece for an AI-literacy program: one concept, one habit.

The problem

Most people either distrust AI answers completely or trust any answer that cites a source. A diagram of RAG teaches the term, but not the habit that matters: checking the source before acting on an answer.

Why this format

  • A chat, not slides. The misconception comes from chatting: a fluent answer feels true. So learners meet it where it happens.
  • One switch, one variable. The same question runs with grounding off and on, so the only difference learners see is what RAG changes.
  • A twist instead of a warning. The last answer cites a real section of the document, but the claim isn’t in it. Learners check the source and find the contradiction themselves, which sticks better than being told that RAG can still hallucinate.
  • Attention on the answers. Prompts are pre-written, a visible cue marks the next step, and staged “searching … reading page 4” states show what retrieval does.

What I’d measure in a real rollout

  • Behaviour, not quiz scores: a scenario check a few weeks later with a new cited answer to verify, and, where the AI tool logs it, how often people open the cited source.
  • Which wrong answer people pick in the source check. “Yes” means they trust citations blindly, “Partly” means they skim. Each points to a different follow-up.
