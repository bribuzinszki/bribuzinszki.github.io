ARTEX: When Security Agents Change Sides

Status: 17 November 2026

EN|DE

Companies have long paid people to break into them. It's called penetration testing, and it's one of the best ways for you to find the holes before somebody else does. In 2026, an open-source framework called ARTEX handed much of that job to a team of AI agents. Within weeks, people it was supposed to protect against started using it, too.

In about ten minutes, you'll see what ARTEX is, how it works, why it keeps making headlines, and what actually protects a company.

First, what's a pentest?

Think of a locksmith you hire to try every door and window of your house. They report back: "The back door's fine, but the old lock on the cellar window opens with an ordinary pocketknife." That's a penetration test, or pentest: an authorised, scoped attempt to break in, with a written report at the end.

Two words carry all the weight: authorised (you said yes) and scoped (you said where). Take them away and the same skills have a different name.

Pentesters have always used tools that automate the boring parts. And attackers have always borrowed those tools. Famous testing tools from the 2000s and 2010s show up in criminal break-ins to this day, and ARTEX follows the same path, just much faster.

So what is ARTEX?

ARTEX is an open-source, multi-agent framework for penetration testing that was published on GitHub in July 2026.

Three words get mixed up a lot in the coverage, so let's pin them down.

Model (LLM)

the engine · swappable

The model is the engine: any existing LLM, just like the one that might write your shopping list or draft your emails. ARTEX doesn't contain one of its own. It plugs into whichever model its operator picks.

An agent is a model that's been given a goal, some tools and a loop. While a chatbot answers a question and then waits for you, an agent breaks its goal into steps, uses its tools, looks at the results and decides what to do next — again and again, without asking you every time.

The framework is the software that runs the agents. ARTEX gives a team of them their roles, their tools and a shared memory, plus a dashboard where a person sets the goal and watches them work.

So ARTEX isn't an AI model, and it isn't a single agent. It's the framework that puts a whole team of agents to work. Google's threat intelligence team, in an October report on ARTEX and frameworks like it, adds one more label: a command-and-control platform. That means it doesn't stop at finding a way in; it also works as a remote control for the machines it gets into.

If you've done Working with AI Agents, each agent here works like Taskzilla — just with a much less friendly job description. And ARTEX runs a whole team of them at once.

How it works: meet the crew

You don't drive ARTEX move by move. You give it a target and a goal in plain language, and the framework hands the job to its team of agents. Reports describe roughly this set-up. First, the agents:

  • a lead that turns your goal into a to-do list and keeps score;
  • planners that break each item into smaller steps;
  • workers that actually run the steps and report back.

And around them, the framework provides:

  • a shared notebook the whole team reads and writes: what the target looks like, what's been tried, what worked;
  • a dashboard with a chat seat, where a human can lean in and nudge the agents at the tricky moments.

The model does the thinking; the framework keeps everyone pointed at the goal and remembers what happened. Swap in a different LLM and the same crew keeps working.

One detail matters for later. The framework's built-in limits — the "don't touch anything outside the target" rules — were mostly instructions to the agents, not locks. Security researchers noted there was no hard technical wall stopping them from colouring outside the lines. Instructions, however, are easy to ignore and just as easy to delete in a copy.

What about the safety rules built into the AI models themselves? They do get in the way, but the reports point to three ways around them. The first is framing: ARTEX presents every job as an authorised penetration test, and a model has no way to check whether that permission is real. Check Point found that the models most operators chose showed no signs of guardrails under that "pentest" framing. The second is splitting the job: the framework breaks a goal into many small steps, so each request the model sees can look like an ordinary piece of work rather than part of a break-in. For example, instead of "Let's break into the neighbour's house", the model gets "Let's cross the street", then "Now walk over to the back door", then "Pick up that brick from the ground". Each step on its own looks harmless, so the guardrails, which would react to the break-in as a whole, often aren't triggered at all. The third is shopping around: if one model refuses, operators switch to a more permissive one.

    From test bench to real attacks

    Here is the interesting part: ARTEX wasn't stolen or cracked. It was posted openly, for anyone, and "anyone" includes people with good and bad intentions alike.

    Within weeks of release, security firms started seeing it in real break-ins. By autumn, security firms had linked it to criminal gangs and to suspected espionage groups, against targets in finance, government, tech, healthcare and more. In October the author took the original project down. It didn't matter much: the code had already been copied, translated, rebranded and bundled into a paid version. As we know, you can't un-publish something the internet has already saved. In November, the FBI put out a public warning.

    So why did a defence tool make such a good weapon? It comes down to three things.

    At a glance

    Three dangers

    1It doesn't care who's holding it

    The skills that find holes for a defender find the same holes for an attacker. Only authorised and scoped changed, and those were just words in a config file.

    2Those soft guardrails came off easily

    When the limit is a polite instruction, a copy can simply remove it.

    3It lowered the bar

    You no longer need deep expertise to run a complex, multi-target operation. You need a goal and a model.

    Why we keep hearing about it

    Attackers repurposing tools is an old story. So why is this one everywhere?

    To see why, picture how a break-in used to work. A serious criminal operation was staffed like a small company: an HR department, coders, testers, system admins, an "offensive team" for the break-in itself and negotiators for the ransom, with team leads and salaries. Agentic frameworks squeeze much of that into software. In 2025, a state-sponsored group used AI agents to do 80–90% of the work in a spying campaign against about 30 targets. Humans stepped in at perhaps four to six key decisions, while the agents fired off thousands of requests, often several per second, a pace Anthropic called "simply impossible to match" for human hackers.

    • Machine speed: A human pentest takes days or weeks. Reports describe ARTEX going from a first foothold to deep access in under three hours, and in one case, from spotting a leaked password to stealing data in five minutes.
    • Scale: One operator can aim it at hundreds of targets at once and let it grind in parallel.
    • A low skill floor: It hands expert moves to non-experts.
    • It's not alone: Researchers already track a small family of similar "agentic offensive frameworks", as Google calls them. ARTEX is the one that broke out, not the only one.

    It's worth naming the trade-off out loud, because the same qualities that make agents useful for good work — they're fast, tireless and need little supervision — are exactly what make them dangerous in the wrong hands.

    Before you unplug the router

    The headlines tend to skip the less exciting half of the story. The same reports that raise the alarm also show where frameworks like ARTEX fall short.

    The other half of the story

    Where it falls short

    It's loud

    Frameworks like ARTEX trade stealth for speed. One team that watched an attack unfold called it "super loud and messy", and the FBI notes the sheer volume of traffic trips alarms. A company that's watching can spot it.

    Protection still changes its plans

    One group pointed it at more than 200 organisations, and told its agents to skip computers running endpoint security. Even an autonomous agent goes looking for the easy way in, so being harder to break into still pays.

    It needs a door that's already open

    No magic tricks: the reported break-ins start with something boring, like a leaked password, a forgotten server or an unpatched system. Close those, and there's far less for it to find.

    Guardrails force a detour

    The FBI says attackers mostly run it on AI models without visible guardrails. In one analysis, the only time a model refused the work, the attacker had to switch to a looser one. Safety work by AI providers adds real friction.

    None of this makes ARTEX, or similar frameworks, harmless, but it does mean the outcome isn't decided by the attacker's tool. It's decided by how many open doors the tool finds, which brings us to what companies can do about it.

    What companies can do

    Does this mean the key to defending against frameworks like ARTEX is getting the basics right? Mostly, yes: so far, the reported break-ins came through known weak spots, such as leaked passwords, missing multi-factor authentication and forgotten systems, not through some unstoppable new trick. But "getting the basics right" isn't the same as "easy".

    The basics now have to cover everything: a framework can check every system you own and every vendor who holds a key, so one forgotten gap is enough. And a known gap can't sit on the to-do list for long, because these tools scan everyone, all the time. Response time is a second challenge. Once someone gets inside, the defence has to move at machine speed: in one reported case, it took five minutes to get from a leaked password to stolen data. Matching that speed by hand is almost impossible, so part of the response has to run on its own.

    Four things to remember

    1. ARTEX is a framework, not a model. It runs a team of agents that plug into whichever model its operator picks. The agents work through the steps on their own; a person sets the goal and only steps in at key moments.
    2. The tool doesn't care who's holding it. A pentest and a break-in differ only in permission and scope. The framework's limits were instructions, not locks, and the models' guardrails can be talked around or swapped out.
    3. It's spreading because it's fast, works at scale and lowers the bar. But it's also loud, and it goes looking for doors that are already open.
    4. What protects a company is mostly the basics, done faster. Close the known gaps, like leaked passwords, missing multi-factor authentication and forgotten systems, including at every vendor who holds a key, and let part of the response run on its own.

    Designer's notes

    What readers should leave with

    After reading, someone can:

    • tell a model, an agent and an agentic framework apart, and explain how an agent differs from a chatbot;
    • say why a tool built for defence ends up in attacks;
    • name three reasons this kind of attack is growing;
    • tell a protection that matters from one that only feels good.

    Who it's for

    A general audience inside a company, non-technical to semi-technical, who keep seeing "AI is hacking companies now" headlines and want to understand what's real without a security background.

    The problem

    The coverage is either breathless ("unstoppable AI hacker") or impenetrable (indicator tables and attack-technique IDs). Neither helps an average employee understand the shape of the thing, or why their company's boring password rules suddenly matter more.

    The idea behind the page setup

    • The page explains the mechanism — goal, plan, act, check, note, repeat — because the mechanism is the transferable idea. It's the same one behind every other agent piece in the portfolio, so the first widget doubles as a reusable explainer. Its engine picker does real work: models refuse now and then, more often in Chatbot mode, where the model reads the whole story, than in Agent team mode, where it only ever sees one small step, so framing, splitting the job and shopping around become something you feel, not just read.
    • Coverage often blurs the model, the agent and the framework. The page keeps them apart from the start, with a small diagram, because "the AI hacked them" hides where the risk actually sits: in what a framework lets its agents do.
    • The "changes sides" framing carries the one idea most worth keeping: capability is neutral; permission is everything. Three illustrated cards turn the three dangers into pictures you can take in at a glance: the same toolbox in two hands, a polite sign a copy simply deletes, and a bar that's dropped to the floor.
    • A page about attacks can easily tip into scaremongering, so "Before you unplug the router" gives the other half of the story from the same reports: these frameworks are loud, they go looking for easy targets, they need a door that's already open, and model guardrails slow them down. One tempting reassurance is deliberately left out: cost. Check Point estimated the AI fees for whole campaigns at roughly 150 to 1,500 dollars, so compute is a bill, not a barrier.
    • The protections come from CERT-EU's advice on ARTEX, and the cards that only feel good are near-misses of that same advice (patching, but monthly; hiding the admin page instead of taking it offline). Recognising security theatre is a more durable skill than memorising a checklist, and near-misses make readers judge each card instead of spotting the silly one.
    • The four takeaways close the main text, and the quick check follows them with one question per takeaway. A wrong answer links back to its section, so the check ties the summary back to the four outcomes above.
    • Conceptual throughout. Nobody should be able to do anything from this page except understand and defend. That's an ethics choice and an audience choice at once; the one hands-on widget uses a locksmith checking a house, never a real technique.

    Honesty note

    The facts come from public reporting that was still shifting as I wrote. Sources disagree on dates and attribution, so the page stays general where they do, carries a status date, and lists its sources. Figures like "under three hours" and "five minutes" are quoted from those reports, not measured by me.
