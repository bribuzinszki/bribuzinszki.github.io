Inference

An interactive learning piece from the course Intro to LLMs, where it follows the lessons on pre-training and post-training. Training produced the model; inference is running it: turning the key and pressing the gas pedal. In about eight minutes, you predict, choose and sample tokens the way a model does, and see where that goes wrong. Short on time? Jump to the designer’s notes.

After this exercise you can:

  • describe inference as next-token prediction in a loop,
  • explain how temperature changes what a model writes,
  • explain why a fluent answer can still be false.

Prompt · pre-written

Context: what the model sees

    Temperature

    What to take away

    1. Inference is a loop. The model predicts one token, adds it to the context and predicts again, until it picks the end token.
    2. It scores, it doesn’t look up. The model ranks every possible next token by probability. It has no answer key, and it doesn’t plan the sentence ahead.
    3. Probable isn’t the same as true. Temperature trades reliability for variety, and even a fluent answer can be a hallucination.

    Designer’s notes

    Who it’s for

    Employees in a company-internal AI-literacy course (Intro to LLMs). It comes right after the lessons on pre-training and post-training, so learners already know what a trained model is.

    The problem

    The original lesson was a click-through: learners pressed “Generate next token” and read a six-step carousel. They saw inference happen, but never made a decision, so the misconceptions that matter most survived: that the model looks answers up, that it plans whole sentences, and that the same question always gets the same answer.

    Why this format

    • Guess first. Learners predict the next token before they see the scores. Committing to a guess makes the probability bars meaningful.
    • The loop in place. Instead of a carousel, a pipeline strip lights up while each token is generated, so the six steps are seen exactly when they happen.
    • You choose, and the story branches. Picking an unlikely token changes every option after it, so “each token becomes context” is experienced, not read.
    • Temperature as a dial. Generating the same story twice at high temperature answers the question learners actually ask: why does the chatbot answer differently every time?
    • A twist to end on. A factual question with a probable wrong answer turns “LLMs have limitations” into a concrete moment, and leads into the RAG exercise.

    What I’d measure in a real rollout

    • Answers to the second quick-check question, as the sharpest test of whether sampling was understood.
    • How many learners pick an unlikely token in the story: a sign they explore instead of clicking through.
    • In a follow-up a few weeks later, whether learners explain an inconsistent chatbot answer with sampling rather than “the AI is broken”.

    Token IDs and probabilities are illustrative, modelled on real tokenizers and models.
